What if the Internet Disappeared for a Year?
[The day it all stopped]
Imagine you wake up tomorrow, reach for your phone, and nothing loads.
Not your Wi-Fi. Not your phone company. The entire internet is gone.
And it's not coming back for one whole year.
Five and a half billion people just lost the first thing they check every morning.
So what would actually happen? On day one, in week one, and over the whole year?
First, the rules. Electricity still works. So do radio, TV broadcasts, and old landline phones.
But anything that sends data over the internet, from apps to bank transfers, simply stops.
Let's start at seven a.m. on the very first morning.
Pip's first move is the same as yours. Restart the router.
Twice. Then a third time, a little more angrily.
Across the street, every neighbor is doing exactly the same thing.
Then Pip switches on an old radio, and hears news nobody expected.
It's global. Every country, every network, all at the same moment.
That's stranger than it sounds. Almost all data between continents travels through cables on the ocean floor.
They carry video calls, card payments, stock trades, even this video.
Today, they carry nothing.

[Chaos on the streets]
By eight a.m., the morning commute falls apart.
Ride apps are dead. Ticket gates won't read phones.
Millions of people realize they don't actually know the way to work without a map app.
GPS satellites still work, by the way. But without data, most maps can't even load the streets.
So people do something they haven't done in years. They ask strangers.

[Cash is king]
Then comes the first real shock. Paying for things.
Card machines need a network to check your bank. No network, no payment.
Some shops can accept cards offline, but only small amounts, and only for a while.
Everyone else needs cash. And everyone needs it at the same time.
Many cash machines run on networks too, so the lines move to the bank branches.
By lunchtime, a handful of cash is the most valuable thing in town.

[Work, shops and flights]
Pip works from home, on video calls. Or rather, used to.
Offices, schools and online stores go quiet. Online shopping alone is worth around six trillion dollars a year.
Every one of those orders just froze, mid-click.
Airports have it even worse. Flights depend on networks for booking, check-in and crew schedules.
We've had a preview. In 2024, one faulty software update crashed about eight and a half million computers and grounded thousands of flights.
That chaos lasted a few days. This one lasts a year.
Hospitals still have power, and doctors still know medicine.
But patient records, lab results and prescriptions live in connected systems.
So they switch to backups. Printed charts, handwritten orders, and runners carrying folders.
By the first night, the city feels strange.
The lights are on. But the windows don't glow blue with screens.
Pip lies in bed, picks up the phone out of habit, and puts it back down.

[Week one]
Week one is when things get serious.
Supermarket shelves start running thin.
Not because the food ran out, but because ordering it ran on the internet.
Stores normally reorder stock automatically, often every day. Trucks follow routes planned by software.
Now managers phone suppliers on landlines, and fax machines wake up from long naps.
Yes. The fax machine is back.
But the bigger problem is information. Without the internet, how do you know what's true?
Rumors fly. It'll be back tomorrow. The banks are closing. Aliens did it.
So people turn to the oldest networks we have. Radio, TV and newspapers.
Printed newspapers, which had been shrinking for years, sell out by nine a.m.
Town halls pin notices to boards, just like a century ago.
But what about talking to people far away?
Most mobile calls now travel as data. Whether they still work depends on how each carrier routes them.
Old landlines hold up better, and lines form at the last few payphones.
And then, something surprising happens. Letters.
The U.S. Postal Service already moves over a hundred billion pieces of mail a year. Now it becomes the internet.
Pip writes a letter for the first time since school. It takes forty minutes. Mostly crossing things out.

[Month one: the new normal]
By the end of the first month, panic turns into routine.
Shops run on cash and paper receipts. Banks process transfers on paper again, slowly.
Companies send couriers on motorbikes between offices, carrying hard drives full of data.
Engineers have a name for this: sneakernet. And for huge amounts of data, a truck full of drives can beat the internet.
Engineers race to build private links between hospitals, banks and power grids.
But rebuilding something the size of the internet? That takes far longer than a year.
So who struggles most?
Online businesses, delivery drivers, app makers and creators lose their income almost overnight.
People far from family feel it most. A weekly video call becomes a letter every two weeks.
And who suddenly thrives?
Libraries. Bookshops. Local markets. Radio stations. Anyone who can fix old machines.
Amateur radio operators, once a quiet hobby, become a lifeline for messages between towns.
And paper maps become bestsellers.

[Month six: getting used to it]
Six months in, something unexpected happens. People get used to it.
Studies of people who quit social media or block mobile internet for a while often find they feel calmer and focus better.
Not everyone, and not right away. But the constant buzzing is gone.
Neighbors talk more, because they have to. Streets fill with swap markets and clubs.
Pip and Momo start a weekly news sheet for their block. Two hundred copies, delivered by hand.
Kids go back to encyclopedias, and argue about facts nobody can look up.
Still, let's be honest. The cost is enormous.
The world economy is built for speed. Without the internet, trade, research and medicine all slow down.
Scientists can't share data instantly. Weather forecasts get less precise.
Many jobs disappear, and millions of people need new ones.
The world doesn't collapse. But it gets poorer, slower, and a lot more local.

[Day 365]
Then, after three hundred and sixty-five days...
A tiny icon appears on Pip's phone. One bar.
The internet is back.
And a year's worth of notifications arrive at once.
Would we go right back to normal? Probably, within a week.
But some things might stick.
Companies would build better backups, so one failure can't stop a hospital or an airport.
People might keep some cash at home, a paper map in the car, and a radio in the drawer.
And Pip? Pip keeps writing letters to Momo. Even though they live next door.
Because the strangest part of a year without the internet wasn't what we lost.
It was what we found when we finally looked up.
Now it's your turn. Could you last one year offline? Or even one week? Tell me in the comments.
And if you were the only person on Earth still online... well, that's a story for next time.
